What Happens If You Can't Afford Care — Every Option Before You Skip Treatment

Payment plans, financial assistance, sliding-scale clinics, and negotiating a bill down. Never just skip needed care over cost without checking these first.

Premium 7 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2026
Key Takeaways
→ Nonprofit hospitals are legally required by the IRS to have a written financial assistance policy — but you have to apply, it isn't automatic.
→ Federally qualified health centers (FQHCs) charge on a sliding scale tied to income, sometimes for a very low fee.
→ Medicaid provides free or low-cost coverage based on income, though rules and eligibility vary significantly by state.
→ ACA Marketplace plans can come with income-based subsidies that lower your monthly premium.
→ You can often negotiate a medical bill directly — asking about a self-pay or cash discount is always worth doing.

Don't Just Skip Care, and Don't Just Ignore the Bill

When money is tight, it's tempting to either avoid going to the doctor at all, or to get a bill and just not deal with it. Both of these are usually the most expensive path in the long run — a treatable problem left alone often becomes a more expensive emergency later, and an ignored bill can turn into a collections account that damages your credit. The good news is that real, legitimate options exist for reducing what you owe — most people just don't know to ask.

This article walks through the main paths worth knowing about, roughly in the order it makes sense to check them: getting care in the first place through low-cost options, reducing a bill you've already received, and handling prescription costs separately if that's the specific issue.

Hospital Financial Assistance — A Real Legal Requirement

Under federal tax law (IRS Section 501(r)), every nonprofit hospital in the country is required to have a written Financial Assistance Policy for patients who can't afford care. This isn't optional or a marketing gesture — it's a condition of the hospital's nonprofit tax status. However, the federal rule doesn't set one fixed income cutoff that applies everywhere; each hospital sets its own policy and income thresholds, so the details vary by hospital. Some states go further and require financial assistance from all hospitals, not just nonprofit ones — check your state's specific rules.

The catch: hospitals generally don't apply this automatically. You typically have to ask for the Financial Assistance Policy and submit an application, often with proof of income. Call the hospital's billing department directly and ask specifically for their "financial assistance" or "charity care" application — using that exact phrase gets you routed to the right department faster than asking generally about a payment plan.

Sliding-Scale Clinics (FQHCs)

Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs) are community health centers that receive federal funding specifically to provide care regardless of a patient's ability to pay. They're required to use a sliding fee scale, meaning what you pay is tied to your income — the lower your income, the less you pay, sometimes for a modest flat fee at the lowest income levels. You can find every FQHC near you using the free official locator tool at findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov — just enter your ZIP code.

Medicaid — Income-Based Coverage

Medicaid provides free or low-cost health coverage to eligible people based primarily on income, though the exact eligibility rules differ by state — some states have expanded Medicaid to cover more low-income adults than others. You can start a Medicaid application through your state's Medicaid agency or through the federal Marketplace at healthcare.gov, which will route you to Medicaid if you appear to qualify based on your answers. You'll typically need proof of identity, residency, income, and household information to apply.

ACA Marketplace Subsidies

If you don't qualify for Medicaid but still need affordable coverage, the ACA Marketplace (healthcare.gov) may offer you a premium tax credit — a subsidy based on your household income that lowers your monthly premium, sometimes substantially. Because eligibility and the size of the credit depend on current income limits that can change, check healthcare.gov directly for up-to-date numbers rather than assuming a fixed figure.

Negotiating a Medical Bill You Already Have

Hospital and provider list prices are often just a starting point, not a fixed final number. A few things worth trying on any bill you're struggling with:

  • Ask for an itemized bill and check it for errors — duplicate charges and billing mistakes are common.
  • Ask directly about a self-pay or cash-pay discount — many providers reduce the bill for patients paying without going through insurance or collections.
  • Ask for a payment plan — many hospitals offer interest-free installment plans if you ask before the bill goes to collections.
  • Ask about the financial assistance policy even after receiving a bill — applying after the fact can sometimes still reduce or eliminate what you owe.
Don't Wait for Collections

The earlier you contact the billing office — ideally before or right after you receive care, rather than after a bill goes to collections — the more options you generally have. Once a bill is sent to a collections agency, it becomes harder to negotiate and can start affecting your credit report.

Be Careful With Medical Credit Cards and Financing Offers

Some providers offer to enroll you in a medical credit card or financing plan on the spot, often promising a period of no interest. Before agreeing, ask specifically what the interest rate becomes if the balance isn't paid in full by the end of that promotional period — with some of these products, if any balance remains when the promotional period ends, you can be charged interest retroactively on the entire original amount, not just what's left. This is very different from a hospital's own interest-free payment plan. Always ask about the hospital's own financial assistance and payment plan options first, since those are frequently offered without any interest at all, before agreeing to third-party medical financing.

If It's Prescription Costs You're Struggling With

Medication costs are a separate issue from a hospital or doctor bill, but similar principles apply: ask the pharmacy or your doctor about lower-cost equivalents (a generic version, for example, when one exists), and ask your doctor's office directly whether the specific manufacturer offers a patient assistance program for that medication — many pharmaceutical companies run these for people who can't afford an ongoing prescription. Community health centers can also often connect patients with lower-cost prescription options as part of their broader sliding-scale services.

Once You're Stable — Plan Ahead for Next Time

If you've gone through the financial assistance or negotiation process once, treat it as a lesson for next time rather than a one-off fix. Save the contact information for your hospital's financial assistance office, keep records of any FQHC or sliding-scale clinic you've used, and revisit your insurance situation (Medicaid, Marketplace subsidies, or employer coverage) at least once a year, since eligibility and options can change as your income or life situation changes.

Quick Reference — Who to Contact

For a hospital bill: the hospital's billing or financial assistance office directly. For a sliding-scale clinic near you: findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov. For Medicaid questions: your state's Medicaid agency, or start at healthcare.gov. For Marketplace coverage and subsidies: healthcare.gov directly.
