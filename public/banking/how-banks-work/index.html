How Banks Work — And How They Make Billions From Your Money

Banks aren't just safe boxes for your cash. They lend it out, charge interest, and profit enormously. Here's exactly how — and what it means for you.

Free 5 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ Your money doesn't sit in a vault — banks lend most of it out to other borrowers
→ Banks profit from the spread between what they pay depositors (~0.5%) and charge borrowers (7–24%)
→ The FDIC insures your deposits up to $250,000 per bank per account category
→ Through fractional reserve banking, banks can lend ~$9 for every $1 deposited
→ Online banks pay 10–50x more interest than traditional banks because their cost structure is lower

What Banks Actually Do With Your Money

Most people picture a bank as a giant vault — you deposit $5,000, they store $5,000, you withdraw it later. That's not how banks work. Not even close.

Banks are financial intermediaries. Their business model is simple: borrow money cheaply from depositors, lend it out at a higher rate to borrowers, and keep the difference. That difference — called the net interest margin — averages about 3% across the US banking industry and generates hundreds of billions of dollars in profit each year.

Here's the gap in stark terms: in 2024, the national average savings account APY at big traditional banks was 0.46%. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was around 7%. Auto loans averaged 8%. Credit cards charged 20–24%. The bank borrows from you at 0.46% and lends at 7–24%. The spread is the business.

Put it in dollar terms: your $5,000 savings account earns $23/year at 0.46% APY. Meanwhile, the bank might lend that same $5,000 to a credit card borrower at 22% — collecting $1,100/year in interest. You get $23. They keep $1,077. That's not a coincidence; that's the model.

Fractional Reserve Banking — How One Dollar Becomes Ten

Here's the part that surprises most people: when you deposit $1,000, the bank does not hold all $1,000 in reserve. They never have. Banks operate on a system called fractional reserve banking — they hold only a fraction of deposits and lend out the rest.

The Federal Reserve historically required banks to hold 10% of deposits in reserve. In March 2020, the Fed reduced this requirement to zero — officially, banks can now lend out 100% of deposits (though in practice they maintain voluntary reserves for liquidity and regulatory capital reasons).

But even at the old 10% reserve requirement, the lending multiplier effect was dramatic. Here's how it works:

The Money Multiplier — How $1,000 Becomes $9,000 in Loans
You deposit into Bank A$1,000
Bank A keeps 10% ($100), lends $900 to borrower$900 lent out
Borrower deposits $900 into Bank B$900
Bank B keeps $90, lends $810 to another borrower$810 lent out
That $810 deposits into Bank C, which lends $729...$729 lent out
Chain continues across the banking system~$9,000 total loans created

Your original $1,000 deposit ultimately supports roughly $9,000 in loans across the entire banking system. This is why banks are so powerful — and why a bank run (everyone withdrawing at once) can be catastrophic. No bank actually has everyone's money on hand at the same time.

This is also why the Federal Reserve exists: as a lender of last resort, it can inject cash into banks during a crisis so that a liquidity shortage doesn't turn into a full collapse.

2. Fees

Banks generate enormous revenue from fees entirely separate from lending. The most notorious: the overdraft fee. When you spend more than your account balance, banks let the transaction go through — then charge you around $35 per overdraft. Sometimes they process multiple overdrafts in a single day. US banks collected over $8 billion in overdraft fees in 2023 alone.

Other common fees include monthly maintenance fees ($10–$15/month at big banks), out-of-network ATM fees ($2.50–$5 per transaction), wire transfer fees ($25–$35), and paper statement fees ($2–$3/month). These are not accidental — they're deliberate revenue streams.

3. Services and Investments

Large banks — especially the big four (JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citibank) — operate massive wealth management divisions, investment banking arms, and insurance subsidiaries. JPMorgan Chase, the largest US bank, reported net income of $49.6 billion in 2023. These numbers come from the full ecosystem of financial services, not just your checking account.

FDIC Insurance — Why Your Money Is Actually Safe

After the Great Depression of the 1930s — when bank panics caused thousands of banks to fail and ordinary people lost their life savings overnight — Congress created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) in 1933.

The FDIC insures your deposits up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per account ownership category. If your bank fails, the FDIC steps in and you get your money back — typically within a few business days. You don't have to file a claim or hire a lawyer. It just happens.

Account categories matter. A single person can have more than $250,000 FDIC-insured at the same bank by spreading across ownership categories: individual accounts ($250K), joint accounts ($250K per co-owner), certain retirement accounts ($250K), and more. If you have significant deposits, this is worth understanding precisely.

Credit unions have equivalent protection through the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA), also up to $250,000. Always verify your institution is insured — you can check any bank at fdic.gov and any credit union at ncua.gov.

Historical Lesson

During the 2008 financial crisis, banks had lent recklessly and nearly collapsed. The FDIC protected depositors, but the crisis showed what happens when banks take too many risks. Washington Mutual — the largest bank failure in US history — was seized by regulators in September 2008. Its depositors lost nothing. Its shareholders lost everything.

What FDIC Does and Does NOT Cover

FDIC insures checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, and CDs. It does NOT cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, annuities, or crypto — even if you bought them through your bank. Keep investment accounts and insured bank accounts separate in your mental accounting.

What This Means for You Practically

Understanding how banks profit directly points you toward better financial decisions. The most actionable one: you should be earning far more interest on your savings.

Online banks — Ally, Marcus by Goldman Sachs, SoFi, Discover — consistently pay 4–5% APY on savings accounts. They can do this because they have no physical branch network. No branch leases, no teller salaries, no armored trucks. Those savings go to depositors instead of overhead. They have the exact same FDIC insurance as Chase or Bank of America.

Traditional big banks pay 0.01% on savings — not because they can't afford to pay more, but because they don't have to. Most people never switch. Your $10,000 sitting in a big bank savings account earns you $1 per year. That same $10,000 in a high-yield online account earns $450–$500 per year. The protection is identical. The difference is entirely in what you choose to do.

Switching takes about 15 minutes online. The money transfer takes 1–3 business days. It is one of the highest-return-per-hour financial moves available to ordinary people.
