How the US Government Works —
The 3 Branches Explained

Congress makes laws, the President executes them, the Supreme Court interprets them. Here's how they check each other — and why it was designed this way.

Free 6 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ The US has 3 branches of government: Legislative (Congress), Executive (President), Judicial (Supreme Court).
→ Each branch has powers to limit the other two — called "checks and balances."
→ Congress has 100 Senators (2 per state) and 435 Representatives (based on state population).
→ The Supreme Court has 9 justices with lifetime appointments — and can strike down any law.
→ This system was designed to prevent any one person or group from gaining too much power.

Why Three Branches — The Philosophy of Separated Power

The Founding Fathers feared concentrated power. They had lived under the authority of a British monarchy and had seen what unchecked power looked like up close. Their solution was radical for its time: deliberately split governmental power into three competing institutions, each capable of limiting the others.

James Madison articulated it clearly in Federalist No. 51: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary." Since neither condition holds, the Constitution engineers a system where ambition checks ambition.

The core idea has two parts. First, separation of powers: the authority to make laws, enforce laws, and interpret laws is held by three distinct institutions — Congress, the President, and the courts. No single branch does all three. Second, checks and balances: each branch has specific tools to constrain the other two, creating a web of mutual accountability that requires cooperation to govern effectively.

This was not accidental. It was the central design choice of the Constitution, and it shapes every political outcome in the United States to this day.

Branch 1: The Legislative Branch — Congress

Congress is the lawmaking branch of the federal government. It is bicameral — meaning it is split into two separate chambers, each with different rules, different terms, and different powers. Both chambers must agree on identical legislation before it can become law.

The Senate

The Senate has 100 senators — exactly 2 per state, regardless of population. Wyoming (576,000 people) has the same Senate representation as California (39 million). This was a deliberate compromise at the Constitutional Convention, giving smaller states equal footing in one chamber.

Senators serve 6-year terms, staggered so that only one-third of the Senate is up for election every two years. This creates stability — even in a wave election year, the Senate can only change by a third. The Senate has exclusive powers not shared with the House: it confirms presidential appointments (Cabinet members, Supreme Court justices, federal judges, ambassadors) and ratifies treaties by a two-thirds vote.

The House of Representatives

The House has 435 members, allocated to states based on population. California has 52 representatives. Wyoming, Montana, Vermont, and Alaska each have just 1. Representatives serve 2-year terms — every single member is up for election every two years, making the House the most directly responsive to public opinion.

The House has one exclusive constitutional power: all revenue and tax bills must originate in the House. It also has the sole power of impeachment — it is the House (not the Senate) that votes to formally charge a president or judge with impeachable offenses.

To pass a federal law, a bill must pass both chambers in identical form. If the Senate and House pass different versions, a conference committee reconciles them, and both chambers must vote again on the unified text.

Branch 2: The Executive Branch — The President

The President is the head of the executive branch — responsible for carrying out and enforcing the laws that Congress passes. The President is elected every four years, not by popular vote, but by the Electoral College (more on that below). The 22nd Amendment limits the presidency to two terms.

The executive branch is massive. Beyond the President and Vice President, it includes the Cabinet — 15 department heads (Secretary of State, Secretary of the Treasury, Secretary of Defense, Attorney General, etc.) — plus dozens of federal agencies, commissions, and roughly 4 million federal civilian employees who implement policy day to day.

Key Presidential Powers

  • Signs bills into law — or vetoes them, sending them back to Congress.
  • Commander-in-Chief of the US military. Only Congress can formally declare war, but the President commands the armed forces.
  • Nominates Supreme Court justices, federal judges, Cabinet members, and ambassadors (all subject to Senate confirmation).
  • Negotiates treaties with foreign governments (requires Senate ratification by two-thirds).
  • Issues executive orders — directives with the force of law that apply within existing legal authority, without requiring Congressional approval. Courts can block executive orders that exceed presidential power.
  • Pardons federal criminals — the President can pardon anyone convicted of a federal crime, with no requirement for Congressional approval.
The Electoral College

The President is elected by the Electoral College — not the national popular vote. Each state gets electoral votes equal to its total Congressional representation (Senators + Representatives). California has 54 electoral votes; Wyoming has 3. There are 538 total. 270 electoral votes wins the presidency. It's possible — and has happened — to win the presidency while losing the popular vote.

Branch 3: The Judicial Branch — The Supreme Court

The Supreme Court sits at the top of the federal judiciary and holds the ultimate authority to interpret the Constitution and federal law. Its most powerful tool is judicial review — the ability to declare any law or executive action unconstitutional, rendering it void and unenforceable. This power isn't explicitly written in the Constitution; it was established by the landmark 1803 case Marbury v. Madison.

The Court has 9 justices: one Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices. They are appointed by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life — or until they resign, retire, or are removed through impeachment. Life tenure insulates them from political pressure; a justice appointed today may shape constitutional law for 30+ years.

Famous Supreme Court Decisions

  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) — Racial segregation in public schools is unconstitutional. Overturned Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).
  • Miranda v. Arizona (1966) — Police must inform suspects of their rights before custodial questioning. Origin of the "Miranda warning."
  • Roe v. Wade (1973) — Established constitutional right to abortion. Overturned by Dobbs v. Jackson (2022), returning the issue to states.
  • Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) — Same-sex marriage is a constitutional right nationwide.

Below the Supreme Court, the federal judiciary includes 13 Circuit Courts of Appeals (which hear appeals from lower courts) and 94 District Courts (the trial-level federal courts where most federal cases begin).

Constitutional Amendment

The Constitution can be amended — but the bar is extremely high. It requires a two-thirds vote in both chambers of Congress, followed by ratification by three-quarters of all states (38 states). Only 27 amendments have ever passed in over 230 years. This is entirely intentional.

How the Three Branches Check Each Other

The system's genius is that no branch can act unilaterally on the most important decisions. Each branch holds specific tools to constrain the others, requiring negotiation and compromise to govern.

Congress checks the President
→ Can override a presidential veto with a 2/3 majority in both chambers
→ Controls the federal budget — the President cannot spend money Congress hasn't approved
→ Can impeach and remove the President (House impeaches, Senate convicts with 2/3 vote)
Congress checks the Judiciary
→ Senate confirms (or rejects) Supreme Court nominees
→ Can impeach and remove federal judges
→ Can propose constitutional amendments to override Court decisions
President checks Congress
→ Vetoes legislation — Congress must override with 2/3 majority in both chambers
→ Can call Congress into special session
→ Pardons federal criminals, limiting Congressional intent in criminal law
President checks the Judiciary
→ Nominates all Supreme Court justices and federal judges
→ Shapes the ideological direction of the courts over decades through nominations
Judiciary checks Congress
→ Can declare any law unconstitutional and void
→ Decision stands unless overturned by a future Court or constitutional amendment
Judiciary checks the President
→ Can strike down executive orders and presidential actions as unconstitutional
→ Courts have blocked multiple presidential executive orders throughout US history

Why This Matters to You Right Now

Every law that directly affects your daily life — tax rates, healthcare rules, minimum wage, drug laws, immigration policy, environmental standards — flows through this three-branch system. The structure isn't abstract civics; it produces the concrete outcomes you live with.

When a president issues an executive order and courts immediately block it, that's not dysfunction — that's the system operating exactly as the Founders designed. When Congress can't agree on a budget and the government partially shuts down, that's deliberate gridlock: the Framers believed the danger of fast, unchecked lawmaking was greater than the cost of slow, contested lawmaking.

Understanding the structure lets you:

  • Know who is actually responsible for specific outcomes — presidents often get blamed or credited for things Congress controls, and vice versa.
  • Understand why change is slow — it requires majorities in two chambers plus presidential signature (or a 2/3 override), each representing different constituencies with different electoral timelines.
  • Recognize when the system is under stress — when one branch overreaches and the others fail to check it, the safeguards built into the Constitution are what stand between ordinary governance and something much more dangerous.
The Electoral College

The Electoral College — not the popular vote — elects the President. Each state gets electoral votes equal to Senators (2) + Representatives. California has 54, Wyoming has 3. 270 of 538 total electoral votes wins. In 2000 and 2016, the winner of the popular vote did not win the presidency.
