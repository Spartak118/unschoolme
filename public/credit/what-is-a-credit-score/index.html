What is a Credit Score —
And Why It Controls Your Life
5 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated May 2026
Key Takeaways
What you'll know after reading this
✓Your credit score is a number from 300–850 that signals your financial trustworthiness to lenders, landlords, and employers
✓FICO and VantageScore are the two main models — both use the same data, slightly different formulas
✓Banks, credit card companies, landlords, phone carriers, and even some employers check your score
✓You can check your score for free every week at AnnualCreditReport.com, Credit Karma, or your bank

What is a credit score?

A credit score is a three-digit number that tells lenders how likely you are to repay money you borrow. It's calculated using your credit history — loans you've had, credit cards, on-time payments, missed payments, and how much debt you currently carry.

Think of it as a report card for your financial life. A high score means you've been responsible with borrowed money. A low score means lenders see you as a risk — and they'll charge you more for it, or refuse you entirely.

758
The average American credit score in 2025 (FICO). Anything above 740 gets you the best interest rates on mortgages, car loans, and credit cards.

Scores range from 300 to 850. Nobody actually has 300 (it would require a catastrophic credit history), and fewer than 1.5% of people hit 850. Most people fall somewhere in the 580–780 range.

Poor (300–579): You'll be denied most credit cards and loans. If approved, interest rates will be very high. Landlords may require extra deposits or co-signers. Some employers won't hire you.

Fair (580–669): You can get approved for basic cards and some loans, but you'll pay higher interest. A subprime car loan at 18% vs. 5% on a $25,000 car costs you $13,000 more over 5 years.

Good (670–739): Most lenders will approve you. You'll get decent rates. This is where most Americans aim to be.

Very Good / Exceptional (740–850): The best rates on everything. Mortgage lenders will compete for your business. This is the zone worth building toward.

Real example: On a $300,000 mortgage at a "Fair" score (620), you might pay 7.5% interest — $2,098/month. At an "Exceptional" score (780), the same loan at 6.0% costs $1,799/month. That's $299/month difference — $107,640 over 30 years, just because of your score.

Who checks your credit score?

More people check your credit than you think. This is why it matters even if you're not taking out a loan right now.

🏦
Banks & Lenders
Mortgages, car loans, personal loans. Your score determines if you're approved and what interest rate you pay.
💳
Credit Card Companies
Every card application triggers a credit check. Better score = better cards, lower APR, higher limits, better rewards.
🏠
Landlords
Most landlords run credit checks on applicants. Below 620 and you may need a larger deposit or co-signer — or get rejected.
📱
Phone Carriers
Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile all check credit for postpaid plans. Low score means prepaid-only or a large deposit.
💼
Some Employers
Jobs in finance, government, or with access to sensitive data often require a credit check. (They need your permission.)
🚗
Car Insurance
In most states, insurers use your credit score to set your premium. Better credit = lower insurance cost.

FICO vs VantageScore — what's the difference?

Two companies dominate the credit scoring world: FICO (Fair Isaac Corporation) and VantageScore. Both use your credit report data and output a 300–850 score. The difference is in their formulas.

FICO: Used by 90%+ of lenders for actual lending decisions. Requires at least 6 months of credit history to generate a score. The industry standard.

VantageScore: Can score you with as little as 1 month of history. More commonly shown on free apps like Credit Karma, Experian, and your bank's app. Great for tracking your progress — but your lender probably uses FICO.

Your VantageScore and FICO score will usually be within 20–30 points of each other. When you see your score on an app, note which model they're showing. If you're applying for a mortgage, ask the lender which FICO version they use — there are dozens of FICO versions for different loan types.

How to check your score for free

You no longer have to pay to see your credit score. Here are the three best free options:

AnnualCreditReport
annualcreditreport.com
The official federally mandated site. Free full credit reports (not scores) from all 3 bureaus weekly.
Credit Karma
creditkarma.com
Free VantageScore from Equifax and TransUnion. Updated weekly. Good for tracking trends over time.
Your Bank
Check your bank app
Most major banks (Chase, Wells Fargo, Bank of America, Capital One) now show your FICO score for free.

Warning: Checking your own credit score never hurts your score — this is called a "soft inquiry." Only when a lender checks your credit during an application does it cause a small, temporary dip ("hard inquiry"). Check your score as often as you want.
