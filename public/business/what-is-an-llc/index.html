What Is an LLC and Why Every
Small Business Needs One

An LLC protects your personal money if your business gets sued. Without one, creditors can come after everything you own personally.

Free 5 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ LLC = Limited Liability Company. "Limited liability" means the business's debts are the business's problem — not yours personally.
→ If your business is sued and loses, only business assets are at risk — your house, car, and savings are protected.
→ LLCs have pass-through taxation — profits and losses flow to your personal tax return. No corporate tax layer.
→ Forming an LLC costs $50–$500 depending on your state, plus annual fees to maintain it.
→ California LLCs pay a mandatory $800/year minimum franchise tax regardless of revenue.

What LLC Actually Stands For

LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. The critical word is "limited" — it refers to your personal liability, your financial exposure if something goes wrong with the business.

When you form an LLC, you create a separate legal entity that can own property, enter contracts, and be sued — independently of you as a person. The business exists as its own thing under the law. That separation is what protects you.

Contrast this with a sole proprietorship: if you operate as a sole proprietor and someone sues your business, they can come after your personal savings, your car, and in some cases your home. There is no legal wall between you and the business. You and the business are legally the same entity.

An LLC builds that wall. It costs a few hundred dollars to create and has saved countless business owners from financial ruin.

The Main Benefit: Protecting Your Personal Assets

Here's the scenario that makes the case for LLCs clearer than any explanation:

Real-World Example — Catering Company LLC
Your business structureLLC
Business bank account balance$5,000
Customer lawsuit amount$200,000
Maximum creditor can take$5,000
Your personal savings at risk$0

Say you run a small catering company as an LLC. A customer gets sick at an event and sues for $200,000. Your LLC only has $5,000 in its bank account. The creditor can take the business's $5,000 — but that's it. Your personal savings, your car, and your home are protected by the LLC's liability shield.

Without an LLC — operating as a sole proprietor — the creditor could potentially pursue your personal assets for the full $200,000.

This protection is why almost every business attorney recommends an LLC for anyone running any business that interacts with customers, provides services, or sells products. The risk doesn't have to feel large for the protection to be worth having.

How LLC Taxes Work

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a "disregarded entity" — the IRS treats it as if it doesn't separately exist for tax purposes. All profit and loss flows directly to your personal tax return on Schedule C. You pay income tax on those profits at your individual tax rate.

You also pay self-employment tax (15.3% on net profit) on top of income tax, because you're both the employer and employee. This is the same as what a sole proprietor pays.

Multi-member LLCs are taxed as partnerships by default, with each member reporting their share of profits and losses on their personal returns.

Here's where it gets strategic: LLCs can elect to be taxed as an S-Corporation (by filing IRS Form 2553). Once you're making roughly $80,000+ in net profit per year, this election can significantly reduce your self-employment tax burden. You pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and take additional profit as a distribution (not subject to self-employment tax). The savings can be substantial — sometimes $5,000–$15,000/year.

Unlike C-corporations, LLCs completely avoid double taxation. Corporations pay corporate income tax, and then shareholders pay again on dividends. LLCs pass everything through to your personal return exactly once.

How to Actually Form an LLC

The process is straightforward and done almost entirely online in most states. Here are the steps in order:

  1. Choose your state. The state where you actually do business is usually the right choice. Ignore advice to form in Delaware or Wyoming unless you have a specific legal or investor-related reason.
  2. Choose a name. Check your state's Secretary of State website for name availability. Confirm it isn't trademarked at tmsearch.uspto.gov.
  3. File Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State. This is the official document that creates your LLC. Most states allow online filing. Cost: $50–$500 depending on state.
  4. Create an Operating Agreement. This document defines how the LLC is managed, how profits are split, what happens if a member wants to leave, and how decisions are made. It's not always legally required (some states mandate it), but it's critically important for multi-member LLCs and good practice for single-member LLCs. Templates are widely available online.
  5. Get an EIN from the IRS. Free, instant, at irs.gov. You need this to open a bank account and file taxes.
  6. Open a dedicated business bank account. Separate finances are not optional — they're what makes the LLC protection real and legally defensible.

What It Costs to Maintain an LLC

Forming an LLC isn't a one-time cost. Every state requires ongoing fees to keep the LLC in good standing. Failing to pay them can result in the LLC being dissolved — eliminating your liability protection.

Registered agent cost: If you pay a service (recommended for privacy), $50–$150/year. You can serve as your own registered agent if you have a physical address in the state and are okay with your address being on public record.

Average total annual cost: $100–$1,000+ depending on your state, with California being the clear outlier due to its $800 minimum franchise tax.

Most Important Step After Forming Your LLC

Open a separate business bank account immediately and NEVER pay personal expenses from it. This single habit is what maintains the legal separation that makes the LLC protection valid. Mix personal and business funds and a court can pierce the corporate veil — ruling the LLC isn't a real separate entity and eliminating your protection entirely.

What an LLC Doesn't Protect You From

The liability shield is real and valuable — but it isn't unlimited. Know these exceptions:

Personal Guarantees

Banks often require you to personally guarantee business loans before approving them. A personal guarantee means exactly what it sounds like: if the business can't pay, you can. The LLC structure doesn't override a written personal guarantee you've signed.

Fraud or Failure to Maintain Separation

Courts can "pierce the corporate veil" — eliminating the LLC's liability protection — if you used the LLC fraudulently or didn't maintain proper financial separation. Paying personal bills from the business account, depositing business revenue into personal accounts, or treating the LLC as an alter ego rather than a real separate entity are all grounds for piercing.

Professional Malpractice

Doctors, lawyers, accountants, and other licensed professionals face a special category of liability: professional malpractice. An LLC generally does not protect you from claims arising from your own professional negligence. Many states require these professionals to use a PLLC (Professional LLC) or PC (Professional Corporation) instead.

Payroll Taxes

If you have employees and collect payroll taxes from their paychecks but fail to remit those taxes to the IRS, you are personally liable — regardless of the LLC structure. The IRS specifically excludes this from entity protection.

Operating in Multiple States

An LLC formed in one state can do business in other states, but you may need to "foreign qualify" — registering as a foreign LLC — in each state where you have a physical presence or significant business operations. Each foreign qualification costs $50–$300 and usually requires a registered agent in that state. If you operate online only, you typically only need to register in your home state.
