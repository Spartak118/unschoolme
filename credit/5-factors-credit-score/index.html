The 5 factors at a glance

Your credit score isn't random — it's calculated from five specific factors, each weighted differently. FICO, the dominant scoring model, breaks it down like this:

Knowing these weights lets you be strategic. If you want to improve your score fast, focus on the top two — they control 65% of your score and both respond to action within 30–60 days.

1. Payment History — 35%

The single biggest factor. Do you pay your bills on time? Every on-time payment strengthens this. Every late payment damages it — severely.

Here's what "late" actually means to the credit bureaus: they don't report a payment late until it's 30 days past due. A payment due on the 1st that you pay on the 29th is technically late to the lender, but it doesn't hit your credit report until the 30-day mark.

Once it hits, the damage is significant: a 30-day late payment can drop a score of 780 by 90–110 points. And it stays on your report for 7 years.

The most important habit in personal finance: Set up autopay for the minimum payment on every account. This guarantees you never miss a due date, even if you forget. Then manually pay the full balance separately to avoid interest.

2. Credit Utilization — 30%

Utilization is the percentage of your available credit that you're using. If your total credit limit across all cards is $10,000 and you currently owe $3,000, your utilization is 30%.

Lower is better. The sweet spot for maximizing your score is keeping utilization below 10%. Above 30% starts to hurt your score. Above 50% is significant damage.

Quick win: Utilization is calculated from the balance reported at the close of your billing cycle — not your actual current balance. Pay down your balance a few days before your statement closes to report a lower number. Results show up next month.

3. Length of Credit History — 15%

This factor considers: how long your oldest account has been open, how long your newest account has been open, and the average age of all your accounts combined.

Older is better. This is why closing your oldest credit card — even one you barely use — is almost always a mistake. You lose that history, which shortens your average account age and can drop your score.

  • Keep your oldest card open, even if unused. Charge one small thing per year to keep it active.
  • If you open new accounts, it pulls down your average age temporarily. Be strategic about when you apply.
  • This factor rewards patience — there's no shortcut, only time.

4. Credit Mix — 10%

Lenders like to see that you can handle different types of credit responsibly. The two main types are:

  • Revolving credit: Credit cards, lines of credit. You can borrow up to a limit, pay it down, and borrow again.
  • Installment loans: Car loans, student loans, mortgages, personal loans. Fixed payments over a fixed term.

Having at least one of each type signals to lenders that you can manage different financial products. That said — don't take on debt just to improve your mix. The 10% weight isn't worth the cost of unnecessary interest.

5. Hard Inquiries (New Credit) — 10%

Every time you apply for new credit — a card, a loan, a mortgage — the lender does a "hard inquiry" on your credit report. This temporarily drops your score by about 5–10 points.

Inquiry TypeStays on ReportAffects Score
Hard inquiry (credit application)2 years~1 year
Soft inquiry (checking your own score)Not shown to lendersNever
Rate shopping (mortgage/auto)Counts as 1 inquiryIf done within 14–45 days

Rate shopping exception: If you're shopping for a mortgage or car loan, FICO counts multiple hard inquiries within a 14–45 day window as a single inquiry. This encourages you to shop for the best rate without penalty. Take advantage of this — compare at least 3 lenders.
