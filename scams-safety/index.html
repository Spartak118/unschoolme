📚 Complete Scams & Safety Guide
What are the biggest warning signs of a phishing message?
Urgency ("your account will be suspended in 1 hour"), a request to click a link and log in immediately, a sender address that's almost-but-not-quite right (like "arnazon.com" instead of "amazon.com"), and any message asking for your password, SSN, or a one-time verification code. Real banks and companies never ask for your password or a login code over text or email. When in doubt, don't click the link — go directly to the company's website or app yourself and check your account from there.
How do fake job offer scams actually work?
A common version: you're offered a remote job with great pay after a very easy "interview," then told you need to buy your own equipment or software through a specific link, or you're sent a check to deposit and asked to wire part of it back for "training materials" — the check later bounces and you're on the hook for the full amount. Red flags: you're hired without a real interview, any request to pay for your own job upfront, and being asked to receive and forward money before you've done actual verifiable work.
How can I tell a real investment opportunity from a crypto or investment scam?
Any offer that guarantees a fixed high return ("earn 20% every week, guaranteed") is not a real investment — all legitimate investments carry risk and none can guarantee returns. Watch for pressure to act fast, apps or platforms you can deposit into but can't easily withdraw from, and social media influencers or DMs from strangers pushing a specific coin or platform. Before putting money anywhere, check if the platform is a registered, regulated exchange, and never invest money you can't afford to lose completely, especially based on a stranger's tip.
What does a typical romance scam look like from start to finish?
It usually starts on a dating app or social media with someone who seems unusually attentive and moves the relationship fast emotionally. They almost always avoid video calls or in-person meetings, citing work travel, military deployment, or similar excuses. Eventually a crisis appears — a medical emergency, a stuck shipment, a visa fee — and they ask for money, often repeatedly and in increasing amounts. The relationship itself is the manipulation tool. If someone you've never met in person or video-called asks you for money, it's a scam, no matter how real the relationship feels.
What should I do in the first 24 hours if I realize I've been scammed?
Contact your bank or card issuer immediately to freeze the account and dispute any charges — most card networks offer fraud protection if you report quickly. Change the passwords on any account you may have exposed. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, for larger losses, file a report with local police and the FBI's IC3.gov for internet crime. Screenshot everything (messages, payment confirmations, usernames) before the scammer can delete or block you, since this evidence matters for disputes and reports.
Why do smart, careful people still fall for these scams?
Scams are specifically engineered to bypass careful thinking by creating urgency, isolation, or strong emotion — fear of losing an account, excitement about easy money, or a genuine emotional connection with a romance scammer. Nobody is immune just because they're smart; the manipulation targets the situation and emotional state, not intelligence. The single best defense is a personal rule: never send money or sensitive information under time pressure — always pause, verify independently, and talk to someone else before acting.
