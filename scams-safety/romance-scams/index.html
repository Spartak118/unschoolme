Romance Scams — How They Work and Why Smart People Fall For Them

Someone online who never video calls, always has an emergency, and eventually needs money. Here's the exact playbook, based on FTC Consumer Advice.

Free 4 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2026
Key Takeaways
→ Romance scammers build a relationship first — the emotional connection is the actual manipulation tool.
→ They consistently avoid meeting in person, often citing military service, overseas work, or a similar excuse.
→ Once trust is built, they ask for money in ways that are hard to trace or reverse — wire transfers, gift cards, or crypto.
→ If someone you've never met in person or video-called asks you for money, that's a scam, regardless of how real the relationship feels.
→ A reverse image search of their profile photo is a fast, free way to check if it's stolen from someone else.

How Romance Scams Typically Start

According to the FTC, romance scams usually begin on a dating app, or with someone reaching out on social media. They move fast emotionally — flooding you with attention, wanting to talk or message every day, and building what feels like a real connection quickly. Scammers frequently use fake profiles built from stolen photos, sometimes even using pictures of real military personnel to seem trustworthy and sympathetic.

These scams can unfold over days, weeks, or even months before any request for money appears. That patience is deliberate — a scammer investing real time into the relationship is building the trust they'll eventually rely on, which is part of why the eventual money request can feel so at odds with everything that came before it.

Why They Always Have a Reason Not to Meet

A defining pattern the FTC has documented: romance scammers consistently avoid meeting in person. Common excuses include claiming to be in the military stationed overseas, doing international business, or working on an oil rig. Sometimes they'll agree to a video call or in-person meeting and then cancel at the last minute, repeatedly. This isn't a coincidence — it's a structural requirement of the scam, since meeting in person or over video would immediately expose the fake identity.

The Money Request — Where the Scam Actually Pays Off

After trust is established — sometimes over weeks or months — the scammer introduces a reason they need money: a medical emergency, a plane ticket to finally visit you, a business problem, customs fees on a package they claim to have sent you. The FTC notes they specifically request payment through methods that are hard to reverse or trace: wiring money through services like Western Union or MoneyGram, loading funds onto gift cards, using payment apps, or transferring cryptocurrency. These payment methods are the tell — legitimate personal or emergency situations don't typically require payment through channels specifically chosen because they can't be undone.

The Simplest Rule

If someone you have never met in person, and have never video-called, asks you for money — it is a scam. This holds true no matter how long you've talked, how real the relationship feels, or how convincing the story is. The relationship itself is the manipulation tool, not a side effect of it.

How to Check If You're Talking to a Real Person

None of these checks require confronting the person directly or accusing them of anything — they're quiet, independent steps you can take on your own time, which matters because a genuine scammer will often have a ready explanation for any single red flag you raise with them directly.

  • Reverse image search their profile photo — if it's associated with a different name or shows up as stock photography, that's a major red flag.
  • Search their claimed job or situation plus "scammer" — the FTC recommends this exact approach, since many scam scripts get reused and reported by previous targets.
  • Push, politely but firmly, for a real-time video call — repeated excuses to avoid this are themselves the answer.
  • Talk to a friend or family member about the relationship before sending any money — an outside perspective often spots red flags you can't see from inside the situation.

Why This Happens to Careful, Smart People Too

Romance scams work by design against emotional connection, not intelligence or carefulness. Isolation, attention, and a sense of being truly understood are powerful, and scammers invest real time building exactly that feeling before ever asking for anything. Nobody is immune simply because they're normally cautious — the manipulation targets the relationship, not a knowledge gap.

A Common Variant: Fake Military Romance Scams

The FTC has specifically warned about romance scammers posing as U.S. service members, often claiming to be deployed overseas. These scammers steal real photos of military personnel to build a convincing fake profile, then use deployment as the built-in excuse for why they can never meet in person or video call. Common requests in this version include money for a "leave request" to come visit, or funds to cover food, communication costs, or medical treatment while supposedly deployed. A more recent twist the FTC has flagged involves scammers claiming to be U.S. troops in a location where the U.S. doesn't actually have a military presence, then asking victims to send money through a fake, official-looking military website to pay for a "care package." Real service members and the actual U.S. military do not ask the public for money this way — if you're ever unsure, you can go directly to official U.S. military or Department of Defense channels to verify a claimed unit or deployment, rather than trusting anything the person themselves provides.

It's Not Just About the Money

Romance scams cause real emotional harm on top of any financial loss — the relationship, even though it was built on a lie, often felt genuine to the person targeted, and losing it can hit as hard as any real breakup, sometimes harder because of the added betrayal and embarrassment. If this happens to you, that reaction is a normal response to a deliberately engineered manipulation, not a sign you were careless or foolish. Talking to a trusted friend, family member, or counselor about what happened — rather than processing it alone — is a reasonable and healthy step, separate from any financial or reporting steps you take.

Watch for a Second Scam Afterward

The FTC has warned that people who've already lost money to a romance scam are sometimes targeted again — this time by someone claiming they can recover the lost funds for an upfront fee. This "recovery scam" pattern is itself fraudulent. Legitimate organizations never charge a fee to help you get scammed money back, and never guarantee a specific result. Treat any follow-up offer like this with the same suspicion as the original scam.

If You Suspect a Romance Scam

Stop sending money immediately, and report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Also notify the dating app or social platform where you met them — most have dedicated reporting tools for exactly this. If you already sent money through a bank, wire service, or payment app, contact that provider right away, since faster reporting sometimes improves the odds of recovering funds, though it's never guaranteed once money is sent.
