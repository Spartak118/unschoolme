📚 Complete College & Financial Aid Guide
What is the difference between a Pell Grant and a student loan?
A Pell Grant is free money from the federal government that does not have to be repaid. For 2024-25 the maximum Pell Grant is $7,395 per year. Eligibility is based on financial need from your FAFSA. A student loan is money you borrow and must repay with interest. Federal Direct Subsidized Loans do not accrue interest while you are in school at least half-time. Always accept grants first, then subsidized loans, then unsubsidized loans. Never take private loans until you have exhausted all federal options.
What is FAFSA and when should I file it?
FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid) is the form used to determine eligibility for federal financial aid. File as early as possible — FAFSA opens October 1 each year for the following school year. Many states and schools award aid on a first-come, first-served basis. Filing late means less aid. You need a student FSA ID and your parent's tax information (or yours if independent). You must file FAFSA every year. Even if you think you won't qualify, file anyway — aid calculations change based on many factors.
What is the real total cost of student loans?
Federal Direct Unsubsidized Loans for undergrads currently charge 6.53% interest (2024-25). If you borrow $40,000 and choose the standard 10-year repayment plan, you pay about $447/month and $53,600 total — $13,600 in interest. On an income-driven repayment plan you pay less monthly but potentially much more total due to extended repayment. Private student loans have rates from 5-17%, no forgiveness options, and no income-driven repayment. The total cost of private loans can be double the borrowed amount over the repayment period.
What is Public Service Loan Forgiveness (PSLF)?
PSLF forgives the remaining balance on federal Direct Loans after 120 qualifying monthly payments (10 years) while working full-time for a qualifying employer. Qualifying employers: government agencies (federal, state, local), 501(c)(3) nonprofits, public schools, public hospitals. You must be on an income-driven repayment plan. After 10 years of on-time payments, any remaining balance is forgiven tax-free. This program is especially valuable for teachers, nurses, social workers, and public defenders with high loan balances.
Is college worth it financially?
The answer depends on: what you study, where you go, and how much debt you take on. The general rule: total student loan debt should not exceed your expected first-year salary. If you expect to earn $50,000/year, borrow no more than $50,000 total. College graduates earn about $1 million more over a lifetime than non-graduates on average, but this varies enormously by major. Engineering, nursing, computer science, and accounting have high ROI. Fine arts, philosophy, and general humanities often do not. Community college + transfer is frequently the smartest financial decision available.
What's the very first step in filing FAFSA if I've never done it before?
Before touching the actual application: create your FSA ID at studentaid.gov and let it verify (1-3 days). Then gather your Social Security Number, your (or your parents') prior-prior year tax return, and current bank balances. Only after you have all of that should you start the FAFSA form itself — starting the form before you have your documents ready is the number one reason first-time applicants abandon it halfway through.
What do I do if the FAFSA asks for my parents' information but I don't live with them?
FAFSA has a specific legal definition of "dependent" that isn't the same as who you live with — it's based on factors like your age, marital status, whether you have children, and whether you're in the military, not living arrangements. Most students under 24 are considered dependent for FAFSA purposes and must include parent information regardless of where they physically live, unless they meet specific independence criteria. If your situation is unusual (estranged from parents, homeless, in foster care), your school's financial aid office can grant a "dependency override" — contact them directly rather than guessing.
What does accepting a student loan offer actually commit me to?
When you accept a loan in your financial aid package, you're signing a legally binding promise to repay it plus interest, typically starting 6 months after you graduate, drop below half-time enrollment, or leave school — whichever comes first. Unlike most other debt, student loans are extremely difficult to discharge in bankruptcy. You can usually choose to accept only part of the loan offered rather than the full amount — borrowing less than what's offered is almost always the smarter move if you can cover the gap another way.
What happens if I can't make my student loan payments after graduating?
Contact your loan servicer before you miss a payment, not after. Options include income-driven repayment (capping payments at a percentage of your income, sometimes $0/month if you earn very little), deferment, or forbearance (temporarily pausing payments, though interest often still accrues). Missing payments without arranging one of these damages your credit significantly and can lead to wage garnishment for federal loans. Never simply stop paying and ignore it — every option is better than default.
