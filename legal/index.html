📚 Complete Legal Guide
What makes a contract legally binding?
For a contract to be legally binding it needs four elements: Offer (one party proposes terms), Acceptance (the other party agrees to those exact terms), Consideration (both parties exchange something of value — money, services, a promise), and Mutual Assent (both parties genuinely agree, not under duress or fraud). Verbal contracts can be legally binding but are much harder to prove. Written contracts are almost always better. Contracts for real estate, loans over $500, and agreements lasting more than 1 year must be in writing to be enforceable.
What is arbitration and should I be worried about it in contracts?
Arbitration is a private dispute resolution process outside the court system. Many contracts — employment agreements, credit cards, app terms of service — include mandatory arbitration clauses that require you to resolve disputes with an arbitrator instead of suing in court. The arbitrator (often chosen by the company) makes a binding decision. Class action lawsuits are usually prohibited. Studies show arbitration outcomes favor companies over consumers and employees. You often cannot opt out. Always read for arbitration clauses and understand what rights you are waiving.
What is the difference between civil and criminal law?
Criminal law involves the government prosecuting someone for violating laws that protect society. The standard of proof is "beyond reasonable doubt." Penalties include prison, fines, probation. You have the right to a public defender if you cannot afford a lawyer. Civil law involves disputes between private parties — lawsuits over contracts, property, personal injury, divorce. The standard is "preponderance of evidence" (more likely than not). Penalties are usually money damages, not prison. You do not have the right to a free lawyer in civil cases.
How does small claims court work?
Small claims court handles relatively small disputes without lawyers (usually). Limits vary: California $12,500, New York $10,000, Texas $20,000. To file: get the defendant's legal name and address, fill out a claim form at your courthouse or online, pay the filing fee ($30-$100), serve the defendant with notice, attend your hearing. Present your evidence: receipts, texts, photos, emails, witnesses. The judge decides same day or within a few weeks. If you win, collecting is a separate process — a judgment does not automatically mean payment.
What is a power of attorney and do I need one?
A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document giving someone else the authority to make decisions on your behalf. A financial POA allows them to manage bank accounts, pay bills, sign contracts. A healthcare POA (also called healthcare proxy) allows them to make medical decisions if you cannot. A durable POA stays in effect if you become incapacitated. Everyone over 18 should have both types — especially before surgery or traveling internationally. You do not need a lawyer; many states provide free forms online. Two witnesses and a notary are usually required.
What actually changes legally the day I turn 18?
You become a legal adult in nearly every state. You can sign binding contracts on your own (leases, phone plans, loans) without a parent co-signing. You can be sued and sued in your own name. Your parents lose automatic legal authority to make medical or financial decisions for you — even they can't access your medical records without your written permission (a HIPAA release) or a signed power of attorney from you. You can also be tried as an adult in criminal court instead of juvenile court.
Can I really be held to a contract I sign the week I turn 18?
Yes, fully. Before 18, most contracts signed by a minor are voidable — the minor can usually cancel them. The instant you turn 18, that protection disappears, and any contract you sign is enforceable against you exactly like an adult, with no special leniency for being new to it. This is exactly why reading the fine print on your first apartment lease, phone contract, or car loan actually matters starting now.
Do I need my own ID and Social Security card once I'm 18?
You should have direct access to your Social Security card, birth certificate, and a state ID or driver's license in your own name — don't rely on a parent holding these for you going forward. You'll need them constantly starting now: renting an apartment, opening a bank account in your own name only, applying for jobs, and filing your own tax return.
Am I automatically responsible for my own medical bills at 18?
Yes — once you're 18, healthcare providers can bill you directly and collections can pursue you personally, even if you're still on a parent's insurance plan (which you can stay on until 26). Being covered by a parent's insurance doesn't mean a parent is legally responsible for your bills anymore; you are. Also, hospitals and doctors generally can't discuss your medical information with your parents without your consent once you're an adult, even in an emergency, unless you've signed a release.
Can I be arrested and tried as an adult now?
Yes. Turning 18 moves you out of the juvenile justice system entirely — you're now processed through the regular adult criminal court system, with adult penalties, a public criminal record (juvenile records are usually sealed), and no automatic right to have a parent present during questioning. Know your rights (the right to remain silent, the right to an attorney) just as seriously as any adult would.
