How to Open a Bank Account —
Step by Step

Checking vs savings, which bank to choose, what documents you need, and how to avoid the fees most people don't see coming.

Free 4 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ Checking = daily spending. Savings = storing and growing money. You usually need both.
→ Online banks pay 4–5% APY on savings vs 0.01% at most big banks — same FDIC protection
→ You need a government ID and SSN (or ITIN) to open a US bank account
→ Avoid accounts with monthly fees, minimum balance requirements, or limited ATM access
→ Credit unions are member-owned nonprofits — often better rates and lower fees than big banks

Checking vs Savings — Know the Difference

These two account types serve completely different purposes, and confusing them costs people money. Most adults need one of each.

A checking account is your financial operating system for daily life. It's where your paycheck lands and where your spending flows out. You use it for debit card purchases, ATM withdrawals, writing checks, paying bills online, and Venmo/Zelle transfers. Checking accounts typically earn 0% interest or near zero — that's fine, because the purpose isn't to grow money, it's to move it. There are no limits on how many transactions you can make per month.

A savings account is where money you're keeping lives. It earns interest while it sits. The federal government historically limited savings accounts to 6 withdrawals per month under Regulation D (though the Fed suspended this limit in 2020, many banks still enforce their own version). The practical rule: don't use your savings account for daily spending. Put money in, let it earn interest, and only withdraw for a specific purpose like your emergency fund or a planned expense.

Open both. Use checking as your everyday wallet. Use savings as a separate bucket you don't touch unless needed. The psychological separation alone helps most people save more.

Big Banks, Credit Unions, and Online Banks — Which Is Right for You?

Where you bank matters more than most people realize. The difference in fees and interest rates between a big bank and an online bank can easily add up to $500+ per year on an ordinary account balance.

Traditional
Big Banks
+ Thousands of ATMs and branches nationwide
+ Trusted brand, long track record
+ Full-service (mortgage, investments, business)
— Monthly fees ($10–$15)
— Savings rates near 0.01% APY
— Minimum balance traps
Nonprofit
Credit Unions
+ Member-owned, profit goes back to members
+ Lower fees, better loan rates
+ NCUA-insured (same as FDIC)
— Must qualify to join
— Smaller ATM networks
— Fewer branches
Digital-First
Online Banks
+ 4–5% APY on savings
+ No monthly fees, no minimums
+ Excellent mobile apps
— No physical branches
— No cash deposits
— No in-person help

The practical recommendation: Use an online bank (Ally, Marcus, SoFi) for savings — you'll earn 4–5% APY instead of 0.01%. For checking, a local credit union or a no-fee online checking account (Chime, Ally) typically beats big banks on fees. If you need in-person banking frequently — cash deposits, notary services, complex transactions — keep a free or low-fee checking account at a big bank, but don't park savings there.

To find a credit union you qualify to join, visit mycreditunion.gov and use their credit union locator tool. Many are open to anyone who lives or works in a certain county, or who is a member of a particular employer or alumni association.

What You Need to Open an Account

Opening a bank account in the US requires a few standard items. Online accounts typically open in 5–10 minutes if you have everything ready.

Documents Checklist
✓
Government-issued photo ID — driver's license, US passport, or state ID card. Must be current and unexpired.
✓
Social Security Number (SSN) — required for identity verification and tax reporting. If you don't have an SSN, an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) is accepted at many banks.
✓
Contact information — current mailing address, email address, and phone number.
✓
Initial deposit — many online banks require $0 to open. Some require $25–$100. Big banks sometimes require more. Check before you apply.
✓
Under 18? A parent or legal guardian must co-sign as a joint account holder. Most banks offer student checking accounts designed for minors.
✓
Non-citizen / no SSN? Some banks accept an ITIN plus a foreign passport. Banks like Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and Chase specifically serve customers without SSNs in many markets.

Note: Banks run a soft inquiry through ChexSystems — a consumer reporting agency that tracks banking history (not your credit score). If you've had accounts closed for unpaid overdrafts or fraud in the past, some banks may decline to open an account. In that case, look for "second-chance" checking accounts specifically designed for people with negative ChexSystems records.

How to Open Your Account — Step by Step

1
Pick your bank
For savings: Ally Bank or Marcus by Goldman Sachs (4.5%+ APY, no fees). For checking: Chime (no fees, $0 minimum), Ally, or your local credit union. If you need branches, Chase offers a free student account with $500+ direct deposit.
2
Go to their website or app
Most accounts open entirely online in 5–10 minutes. You do not need to visit a branch. Have your ID and SSN ready before you start — applications time out.
3
Enter your personal information
Full legal name, date of birth, current address, SSN, and contact info. This information is used for identity verification under federal Know Your Customer (KYC) requirements — it's mandatory, not optional.
4
Choose your account type
Select checking, savings, or both. Opening both at the same bank is usually seamless and makes transferring money between them instant.
5
Fund the account
Link an existing bank account and initiate a transfer (1–3 business days). Alternatively, use a debit card for instant funding if the bank allows it. For in-person banks, you can bring cash or a check.
6
Set up direct deposit
Give your employer your new routing number and account number — both are visible in your account dashboard or on your first check. Most employers update direct deposit within 1–2 pay cycles.
7
Order your debit card and set alerts
Request your debit card (arrives in 7–10 business days for most banks; Chime and some others offer expedited shipping). Immediately set up low-balance text alerts — this single step prevents most overdraft situations.
The Math That Should Make You Switch

$10,000 at 0.01% APY (big bank average) earns $1 per year. The same $10,000 at 4.5% APY (online bank) earns $450 per year. Both accounts have identical FDIC protection. Switching takes 15 minutes online. That's $449 for 15 minutes of work — more than $1,796/hour.

Watch Out For This

Big banks often require a $1,500 minimum daily balance to waive their monthly maintenance fee. Drop below $1,499 for even one day and they charge you $12–$15. Read the full fee schedule — specifically the "account maintenance fee" section — before opening any account. If it's not clearly $0, ask how to waive it permanently.
