DACA Explained —
What It Is, Who Qualifies, and What It Gives You

DACA protects hundreds of thousands of young immigrants from deportation and lets them work legally. Here's what it does, what it doesn't do, and how to keep it current.

Free 6 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ DACA gives eligible immigrants a 2-year renewable work permit and protection from deportation — not a green card or citizenship.
→ DACA recipients can get a Social Security Number, driver's license, open bank accounts, and work legally.
→ Renewals must be filed before expiration — do not wait for the last minute.
→ Only use official USCIS.gov. Never pay an unauthorized person or "notario" to file for you.
→ DACA's legal status is currently in active litigation — stay informed through official sources.

What Is DACA?

DACA stands for Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals. It is a federal policy created by executive action in June 2012 that allows certain undocumented immigrants who came to the United States as children to receive protection from deportation and the ability to work legally in the US.

The program does not provide lawful immigration status, a green card, or any path to citizenship. It is a deferral — meaning the government agrees to postpone deportation proceedings for approved applicants for a 2-year period, which can be renewed indefinitely.

As of 2025, approximately 530,000 people hold active DACA status. They are sometimes called "Dreamers," a reference to the DREAM Act legislation that was proposed but never passed by Congress.

Who Qualifies for DACA?

To be eligible, you must meet all of the following requirements as established by USCIS:

  • Age of entry: You must have come to the US before your 16th birthday
  • Continuous residence: You must have continuously resided in the US since June 15, 2007
  • Physical presence: You must have been physically present in the US on June 15, 2012, and at the time of filing
  • No lawful status: You must have been without lawful immigration status on June 15, 2012
  • Age: You must have been under age 31 on June 15, 2012
  • Education or military: You must be in school, have graduated high school or earned a GED, or have been honorably discharged from the military
  • No serious criminal record: You must not have been convicted of a felony, significant misdemeanor, or three or more misdemeanors
New Applicants

Due to ongoing court litigation, USCIS is currently only accepting renewal applications from existing DACA recipients — not new initial applications from people who never had DACA before. This situation is subject to change. Check uscis.gov/DACA for current status.

What DACA Actually Gives You

When approved, DACA gives you a physical document called an Employment Authorization Document (EAD), sometimes called a work permit. With an active EAD, you can:

BenefitDetails
Work legallyAny employer, any job, any state
Social Security NumberIssued with your EAD — can build credit history
Driver's licenseAvailable in all 50 states for DACA holders
Open bank accountsChecking, savings, credit cards
Pay taxesFile with SSN or ITIN; builds Social Security credits
In-state college tuitionAvailable in most states; check state-specific rules
Travel (advance parole)Possible with separate advance parole application

DACA does NOT give you: lawful permanent resident status, a path to citizenship, the ability to vote, or protection from all immigration enforcement. If you travel internationally without advance parole approval from USCIS, you risk being unable to return.

How to Apply or Renew

All DACA applications are handled by USCIS (US Citizenship and Immigration Services). The only official website is uscis.gov. Do not use any other website claiming to process DACA applications.

What You File

To apply or renew DACA, you submit:

  1. Form I-821D — Consideration of Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals
  2. Form I-765 — Application for Employment Authorization
  3. Form I-765WS — Worksheet supporting the I-765
  4. Filing fee: $495 (covers both forms — no fee waiver available for renewals)
  5. Supporting documents: identity, arrival in US, continuous residence, education/military status

When to Renew

USCIS recommends filing your renewal application 150–120 days before your current DACA expires. Do not wait until 90 days or less — processing times can exceed 90 days. If your DACA expires before you receive your renewal, your work authorization also expires, and you must stop working until you receive the new EAD.

Check Processing Times

USCIS processing times for DACA renewals vary. Check the current processing times at egov.uscis.gov/processing-times. You can also check your case status online with your receipt number after filing.

Protect Yourself: Notario Fraud

In many Latin American countries, a "notario" (notary) is a trained legal professional. In the United States, a notary public is simply someone authorized to witness signatures — they are NOT attorneys and cannot give immigration legal advice or file immigration applications on your behalf.

Notario fraud is widespread in immigrant communities. People pay hundreds or thousands of dollars to unauthorized "immigration consultants" who file incorrect paperwork, steal fees, or never file at all — leaving applicants worse off than before, sometimes with a deportation order.

Only work with:

  • A licensed immigration attorney (find one at ilrc.org or aila.org)
  • An accredited representative at a recognized nonprofit organization
  • USCIS directly (free) at uscis.gov
Free or Low-Cost Legal Help

Many nonprofit organizations offer free or sliding-scale DACA assistance. Search for "DACA legal help near me" or visit the Immigrant Legal Resource Center at ilrc.org to find accredited organizations in your area. Do not pay for help you can get free from a nonprofit.
