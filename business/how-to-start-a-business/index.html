How to Start a Business in the US —
Complete Beginner's Guide

Name, structure, registration, EIN, bank account. Every step to go from idea to legal business, explained in the right order.

Free 6 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2025
Key Takeaways
→ You can start a legal business in the US in a few days — most of it is done online.
→ The most important first decision: sole proprietorship vs LLC — and LLC is usually worth it.
→ Your EIN (Employer Identification Number) is free from the IRS and takes minutes to get.
→ Always open a separate business bank account — mixing personal and business money is a costly mistake.
→ Most businesses also need a local business license from their city or county.

Step 1: Choose Your Business Structure

The structure you choose affects taxes, liability, and paperwork — so this is the decision that shapes everything else. Get it right from the start and the rest of the process is straightforward.

Sole Proprietorship

The default. No registration required — you are the business. The moment you start selling something as an individual, you're technically a sole proprietor. Downside: zero liability protection. If your business is sued, your personal assets — savings, car, home — can all be seized. Good only for very low-risk freelance or side work where the chance of a lawsuit is genuinely negligible.

LLC (Limited Liability Company)

Best for most small businesses. An LLC creates a legal wall between you and your business — protecting your personal assets from business debts and lawsuits. It uses pass-through taxation, meaning profits flow to your personal tax return and are taxed as personal income (no corporate tax layer). You file Articles of Organization with your state, which costs $50–$500 depending on where you live. Highly recommended for anyone starting a real business.

S-Corporation

Good for businesses generating $80,000+/year in net profit where you want to reduce self-employment taxes. With an S-Corp, you pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and take the rest as a distribution (not subject to self-employment tax). More complex — requires payroll, strict rules about shareholders, and ongoing compliance. Usually set up after you've proven the business works and have a CPA advising you.

C-Corporation

For venture-backed startups seeking investment. Investors (VCs, angels) typically require a C-Corp structure. It's complex, expensive to maintain, and subject to double taxation — the corporation pays corporate tax, then shareholders pay again on dividends. Don't start here unless you know you need it.

Recommendation for most beginners: Start as an LLC. If your idea is truly experimental and low-risk, start as a sole prop and convert to an LLC once you have revenue.

Step 2: Name Your Business

Your business name needs to pass three tests before you commit to it: it must be available in your state, not trademarked by someone else, and available as a domain name. Skipping any of these checks causes problems later.

Where to check:

  • State business name database — usually on the Secretary of State website. Search your state + "business name search."
  • USPTO trademark database — tmsearch.uspto.gov — check that your name isn't federally trademarked.
  • Domain availability — namecheap.com or godaddy.com. Even if you don't build a website immediately, secure the domain. A .com costs ~$12/year.

DBA (Doing Business As): If you want to operate under a different name than your legal entity — for example, your LLC is "Smith Holdings LLC" but you want to be known as "Smith Plumbing" — you'll file a DBA (also called a fictitious business name or trade name) at your county clerk's office. Cost: usually $10–$30.

Step 3: Register Your Business

For sole proprietors, no state registration is required in most cases — just get local licenses (covered in Step 6).

For LLCs and corporations: you file with your state's Secretary of State office. This is almost always done online. You'll file:

  • Articles of Organization (for an LLC)
  • Articles of Incorporation (for a corporation)

Filing costs by state: $50 in Kentucky to $500 in Massachusetts. California charges $70 to file plus a mandatory $800/year minimum franchise tax — making it one of the most expensive states for LLCs.

You'll also need a Registered Agent — someone with a physical address in your state who can legally receive documents (lawsuits, government notices) on behalf of your business. You can serve as your own registered agent or pay a service. Registered agent services cost $50–$150/year and are worth it for privacy (your home address stays off public records).

Important Advice

Register your business in the state where you actually operate — not in Delaware or Wyoming unless you have a specific reason. Those states are popular myths for small businesses. They often add complexity and extra cost (foreign qualification fees, dual registered agents) without delivering real benefits unless you're seeking investment or have specific legal needs.

Step 4: Get Your EIN — It's Free and Takes 5 Minutes

Your EIN (Employer Identification Number) is like a Social Security Number for your business. The IRS uses it to track your business's tax obligations. You need it to:

  • Open a business bank account
  • Pay employees or contractors
  • File business taxes
  • Apply for business credit cards and loans
  • Apply for business licenses in some jurisdictions

How to get it: Go directly to irs.gov — search "Apply for an EIN online." Fill out the form on the IRS website. Receive your EIN instantly in PDF form. The entire process takes about five minutes.

It is completely free. There are companies that charge $50–$200 to "get your EIN for you." This is unnecessary. Never pay a third party for a service the IRS provides free in five minutes.

Step 5: Open a Business Bank Account

This step is non-negotiable. Mixing personal and business finances creates three serious problems:

  • It makes bookkeeping a nightmare — you'll spend hours separating transactions come tax time.
  • It can destroy your LLC's liability protection. Courts call this "piercing the corporate veil." If you pay personal bills from the business account or deposit business income into your personal account, a court can rule that the LLC is not a real separate entity — eliminating the protection you paid to set up.
  • It makes it impossible to accurately track business expenses and deductions, costing you money at tax time.

What to look for in a business bank account: no monthly fees, no minimum balance requirements, free ACH transfers, good mobile app, and ideally a debit card.

Options worth considering:

  • Relay — excellent free online business banking, no fees, multiple accounts, good for organized bookkeeping.
  • Bluevine — free business checking that earns interest on your balance.
  • Chase Business Complete Checking — good if you need physical branches and in-person deposits.

To open the account, you'll typically need your EIN, your Articles of Organization (or Articles of Incorporation), a photo ID, and an initial deposit (often $0–$25).

California Warning

California's $800/year minimum franchise tax applies to ALL LLCs doing business in California — even if you don't make a single dollar of profit. This is separate from income taxes. Factor this into your decision if you're based in California.

Step 6: Licenses and Permits

Most businesses need a local business license — even home-based ones. This is separate from your state LLC registration and is required by most cities and counties to legally operate within their jurisdiction.

Where to get it: your city or county clerk's office, or their website. Search "[your city] business license." Cost: typically $50–$150/year.

Industry-Specific Permits

Depending on what you do, you may need additional licenses:

  • Food service: health department permit, food handler's certification
  • Contractors: state contractor's license (varies by trade and state)
  • Childcare: state licensing and background checks
  • Professional services (accounting, law, medicine): professional license from your state's licensing board
  • Selling alcohol: state liquor license plus possible federal permits

Federal Licenses

Very few businesses need federal licenses. Exceptions include alcohol production, tobacco, firearms, broadcasting, aviation, and a handful of other regulated industries. For most small businesses, federal registration is not required. The SBA maintains a full list at sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/apply-licenses-permits.
