How to Read a Lease Before You Sign

A lease is a binding legal contract, not a formality. Here's every clause you need to understand before you hand over a deposit.

Free 6 min read UnschoolMe Editorial Updated 2026
Key Takeaways
→ A lease is a legally binding contract — verbal promises that aren't written into it don't count.
→ In California, security deposits are capped at 1 month's rent for unfurnished units and must be returned within 21 days with an itemized list.
→ Landlords must give 24 hours' advance notice before entering in California, and must maintain a habitable unit.

→ Renters insurance costs $15-30/month and protects both your belongings and you against liability.

What a Lease Actually Is

A lease is a legally binding contract between you and a landlord that sets the rules for renting a property — rent amount, duration, responsibilities, and what happens if either side breaks the agreement. Once you sign it, courts will generally enforce exactly what's written, regardless of what was said verbally during the apartment tour. If your landlord promised something that matters to you — permission to have a pet, a specific move-in date, a repair they'll complete — get it added to the lease in writing before you sign.

Most leases run 12 months, though month-to-month agreements exist too. Read the entire document, not just the highlighted signature line. Leases are usually 5-15 pages, and the clauses that cause the most disputes are often buried in the middle.

Security Deposit Rules

In California, a security deposit for an unfurnished unit cannot exceed one month's rent (two months for furnished units, as of recent state law changes for most landlords). The deposit must be returned within 21 days of you moving out, along with an itemized statement explaining any deductions.

If your landlord fails to return your deposit or the itemized list within the deadline, many states — including California — allow you to sue for up to two to three times the withheld amount in small claims court.

Landlord Entry and Your Privacy

Your landlord does not have unrestricted access to your home just because they own the building. In California, landlords must give at least 24 hours' advance written notice before entering, except in genuine emergencies (a burst pipe, a fire). Entry must also happen during reasonable hours, typically business hours. If your landlord repeatedly enters without notice, document each instance with dates and send a written complaint.

Subletting and Early Termination

Check the lease's subletting clause before you sign — some leases ban subletting entirely, others allow it with the landlord's written approval. If your plans might change during the lease term (a job relocation, a breakup, financial hardship), check the early termination clause. It should specify the exact fee or notice period required to break the lease early. Without this clause, breaking a lease can mean owing rent for the remaining months.

Rent Increase Notice Requirements

Landlords can't raise your rent whenever they want, even on a month-to-month agreement. In California, a landlord must give 30 days' notice for a rent increase under 10%, and 90 days' notice for an increase of 10% or more. Statewide rent control (AB 1482) also caps annual increases at 5% plus local inflation, up to a maximum of 10% total, for many older buildings. Check whether your unit is covered.

The Implied Warranty of Habitability

Every rental in the US comes with an automatic legal guarantee, even if it's not written in the lease: the unit must be genuinely livable. This means working heat, hot water, plumbing, weatherproofing, and no pest infestations. If something breaks, the landlord must fix it within a reasonable time after you notify them in writing. If they don't, tenants may have the right to withhold rent, "repair and deduct" the cost from rent, or terminate the lease — but the exact process varies significantly by state, so check your local tenant rights rules before acting.

Do This the Day You Move In

Walk through the entire unit and take timestamped photos and video of every room, every wall, every appliance, and every existing scuff or stain. Send them to your landlord in an email so there's a dated record. This single habit is the strongest protection you have when it's time to get your deposit back.

Get Renters Insurance

For roughly $15-30 per month, renters insurance covers your personal belongings against theft, fire, and water damage, and covers liability if someone is injured in your unit. Your landlord's insurance only covers the building structure — never your possessions. Many landlords now require proof of renters insurance as a lease condition, but even when it isn't required, it is almost always worth the cost.

If Your Landlord Violates the Lease

If your landlord fails to make required repairs, enters without notice, or violates any other term, start by sending a clear, dated written notice (email or certified mail) describing the issue and requesting it be resolved within a specific number of days. Keep copies of everything. If the issue isn't resolved, contact a local tenant rights organization or legal aid clinic — many offer free consultations and know your state and city's specific protections far better than a general guide can.
