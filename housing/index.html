📚 Complete Housing Guide
What can a landlord legally deduct from my security deposit?
Landlords can deduct for: unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, cleaning if the unit is left unusually dirty, and sometimes early termination fees specified in the lease. They CANNOT deduct for: normal wear and tear (faded paint, worn carpet from normal use), pre-existing damage, or damage they failed to disclose. Most states require landlords to return the deposit with an itemized deduction list within 14-30 days. In California the limit is 21 days. If they fail, many states allow you to sue for double or triple the deposit amount.
What is the eviction process and can I be removed immediately?
No — you cannot be legally removed from your home without going through the formal eviction process. The process typically takes 30-90 days. Steps: 1. Landlord serves written notice (3-day notice to pay or quit, 30-day notice to vacate, etc). 2. If you don't comply, landlord files an unlawful detainer lawsuit in court. 3. You are served court papers and have the right to respond. 4. A hearing is scheduled. 5. If the landlord wins, the court issues a writ of possession. 6. A sheriff enforces the writ. Only a sheriff can physically remove you — not the landlord.
What is a mortgage and how does interest work on a home loan?
A mortgage is a loan secured by the property itself. If you stop paying, the lender can foreclose and take the home. On a 30-year fixed mortgage at 7%, you pay a fixed monthly payment for 360 months. In the early years, most of your payment goes toward interest. On a $400,000 loan at 7%, your monthly payment is about $2,661. Over 30 years you pay $558,000 total — $400,000 principal plus $158,000 in interest. A 15-year mortgage has higher payments but you pay much less total interest. A 1% lower rate saves tens of thousands over the life of the loan.
What is the 30% rent rule and is it still realistic?
The traditional rule is to spend no more than 30% of your gross income on rent. If you earn $60,000 per year ($5,000/month), the rule suggests maximum rent of $1,500. Many housing experts now say this rule is outdated given current housing costs, especially in expensive cities. A more practical approach: spend no more than 30% of your NET (take-home) pay on rent, and keep total housing costs (rent + utilities + renters insurance) under that threshold. If you must spend more, reduce other expenses — particularly discretionary spending.
What is renters insurance and do I need it?
Renters insurance covers your personal belongings (furniture, electronics, clothes) against theft, fire, water damage, and other perils. It also covers liability — if someone is injured in your apartment, your policy pays their medical bills and legal costs. Average cost: $15-$30 per month for $30,000 in personal property coverage and $100,000 in liability. Your landlord's insurance only covers the building, not your stuff. Many landlords now require it. Even if not required, the monthly cost of renters insurance is almost always worth it — a single theft or fire can wipe out everything you own.
What does it actually cost to move out for the first time, beyond just rent?
Budget for all of this before you sign anything: security deposit (usually 1 month's rent), first month's rent, sometimes last month's rent upfront, application/screening fees ($30-75 per applicant), and often a pet deposit if you have one. For a $1,200/month apartment, moving-in costs can realistically total $2,500-$3,600 before you've bought a single piece of furniture. Add $500-1,500 for basic furniture and setup, plus utility deposits if you have no billing history in your name yet.
What's the difference between first month's rent, last month's rent, and a security deposit?
First month's rent is simply payment for your first month living there. Last month's rent (not all landlords require this) is collected upfront and applied automatically to your final month, so you don't pay rent that last month. A security deposit is separate and is meant to cover damage or unpaid rent — it's refundable, while last month's rent is not a refund, it's a prepayment. Always ask your landlord to clarify which of these they're actually charging, since the terms get mixed up constantly.
How should roommates split rent fairly when rooms aren't the same size?
The simplest fair method: split rent proportionally to each bedroom's square footage, or by a flat premium for the larger/master room (commonly $100-200 extra per month for a bigger room or private bathroom). Shared spaces (kitchen, living room) are considered equal-value for everyone. Get the split in writing — a simple shared note or spreadsheet — before move-in, not after a disagreement starts.
Should all roommates be on the lease, and what happens if only one person is?
Whenever possible, every roommate should be listed on the lease. If only one person signs, that person is the only one legally responsible to the landlord — and the only one with legal rights if there's a dispute. Unlisted roommates have no formal standing with the landlord and can be asked to leave without notice in some situations. If a landlord won't add everyone to the lease, get a separate written roommate agreement between yourselves covering rent splits and move-out terms.
What do we do if a roommate stops paying their share?
Address it immediately and in writing (text or email) — don't let it slide for "just this month," since it tends to repeat. If your names are jointly on the lease, the landlord can hold everyone responsible for the full rent regardless of the internal split, so unpaid roommate rent becomes everyone's problem fast. Small claims court is an option to recover a roommate's unpaid share after they've moved out, but preventing it with a clear written agreement upfront is far easier than collecting after the fact.
